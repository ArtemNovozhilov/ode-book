Section

On extending solutions

Chapter 2 · Fundamental theorems

Theorem 2.42 is local. In this section I will discuss how the solutions to (2.7) can be extended beyond the established interval. Everywhere in this section I assume that the conditions of Theorem 2.42 hold. I start with an almost obvious lemma, which, however, will allow me to extend my local solution to the largest possible interval of \(t\).

Lemma 2.45. Let \(\bs x\) and \(\bs y\) be two solutions to (2.7) defined on intervals \(I_{\bs x}\) and \(I_{\bs y}\). Then \(\bs x(t)=\bs y(t)\) for all \(t\in I_{\bs x}\cap I_{\bs y}\).

Proof. Assume that \(\bs x(t)\neq\bs y(t)\) for some \(t>t_0\) (the case \(t<t_0\) is similar), and let \(t^\ast\) be the supremum of those \(s\) for which \(\bs x=\bs y\) on \([t_0,s]\). By continuity, \(\bs x(t^\ast)=\bs y(t^\ast)\). Applying the main theorem with the initial condition at \(t^\ast\), I get that \(\bs x=\bs y\) in a neighborhood of \(t^\ast\), which contradicts the definition of \(t^\ast\). ■

Definition 2.46. A solution to (2.7) is called maximal if it cannot be extended to a solution on a larger interval. A maximal solution is called global if its domain coincides with \(J\).

To make sure that my definition makes sense, I must show that a maximal solution always exists. This is the next theorem.

Theorem 2.47. Problem (2.7) has a unique maximal solution. It is defined on an open interval \((\alpha,\beta)\), where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) may be infinite.

Proof. Let \(I\) be the union of the intervals of all solutions to (2.7), and for \(t\in I\) set \(\bs x(t)\) to be the value at \(t\) of any solution defined at \(t\). By Lemma 2.45 this does not depend on the choice of the solution, and \(\bs x\) is a solution on \(I\) that, by construction, cannot be extended. Any other maximal solution coincides with \(\bs x\) by Lemma 2.45.

If \(I\) contained its right endpoint \(\beta\), then, since \((\beta,\bs x(\beta))\in U\) and \(U\) is open, Theorem 2.42 with the initial condition at \(\beta\) would extend \(\bs x\) beyond \(\beta\). Hence \(I\) is open. ■

Next, I would like to understand what happens to the maximal solution to (2.7) when \(t\to\beta\) or \(t\to\alpha\). This will be the second fundamental result of this chapter. I will look only at the interval \([t_0,\beta)\); the other half can be handled similarly.

Theorem 2.48. In the setting of the existence and uniqueness theorem let \(U\) be open, and let \(K\subseteq U\) be compact. Let \(t\mapsto \bs \phi(t)\) be the maximal solution through the point \((t_0,\bs x_0)\), defined on \((\alpha,\beta)\). Then there exists \(t_K<\beta\) such that \((t,\bs \phi(t))\notin K\) for all \(t\in(t_K,\beta)\).

(In words: the graph of the maximal solution eventually leaves any compact set and never returns.)

Proof. I will prove this theorem by contradiction. So, assume that \(\bs \phi\) does not leave \(K\) for good.

\((i)\) That means that there exists a sequence \(t_n\to\beta\) such that \((t_n,\bs\phi(t_n))\in K\), and hence \(\beta\) is finite.

\((ii)\) Since \(K\) is compact and \(U\) is open, there is a positive distance between \(K\) and the boundary of \(U\) (to be precise, \(\operatorname{dist}(K,\R^{k+1}\setminus U)>0\) when \(U\neq \R^{k+1}\)). This implies that it is always possible to find \(r>0\) such that the set \(K_r=\{(t,\bs x)\colon\ {\lvert t-\tau\rvert }\le r,\ {\lvert \bs x-\bs \xi\rvert }\le r \text{ for some } (\tau,\bs \xi)\in K\}\) lies in \(U\). The set \(K_r\) is compact. Let \(M=\max_{K_r}{\lvert \bs f\rvert }\). For every \((\tau,\bs \xi)\in K\) the cube of half-side \(r\) around it lies in \(K_r\), so Theorem 2.42 gives a solution through \((\tau,\bs \xi)\) on \([\tau-\delta,\tau+\delta]\) with \(\delta=\min\{r,\ r/M\}\), which does not depend on \((\tau,\bs \xi)\).

\((iii)\) Now pick \(t_n\) from the sequence such that \(t_n>\beta-\delta\), which is possible due to \((i)\) and \((ii)\). The point \((t_n,\bs\phi(t_n))\) belongs to \(K\), hence, by \((ii)\), there is a solution \(\bs \psi\) on \([t_n-\delta,t_n+\delta]\) with \(\bs \psi(t_n)=\bs \phi(t_n)\). By Lemma 2.45, \(\bs\psi\) and \(\bs\phi\) coincide at least on \((t_n-\delta,\beta)\).

\((iv)\) Now construct the function \[ \bs \sigma(t)=\begin{cases}\bs \phi(t),&t\in(\alpha,\beta),\\ \bs \psi(t),&t\in[t_n,t_n+\delta). \end{cases} \] By construction \(\bs \sigma\) is \(\mathcal C^{(1)}\) and solves (2.7) on \((\alpha,t_n+\delta)\). This contradicts the maximality of \(\bs\phi\). ■

Now I state several important corollaries of this theorem.

Corollary 2.49. Let \(U=\R\times X\) (i.e., \(\bs f\) is defined for all \(t\)). If \(\bs \phi\) is the maximal solution to (2.7) on \([t_0,\beta)\) and \(\bs \phi\) stays in a compact subset \(K\) of \(X\), then \(\beta=+\infty\).

Proof. Assume that \(\beta\) is finite and use the compact set \([t_0,\beta]\times K\subseteq U\). The theorem says that the graph of \(\bs \phi\) must leave this set, but by assumption it stays inside. A contradiction. ■

The previous corollary treats only the forward interval \([t_0,\beta)\); a similar fact clearly holds for \((\alpha,t_0]\). A useful rephrasing (taking into account both directions of \(t\)): if for the autonomous problem \(\dot{\bs x}=\bs f(\bs x),\,\bs x(t)\in X\), the solutions never leave a compact set \(K\subseteq X\), they must be global.

Corollary 2.50. Assume \(U=J\times \R^k\), \(J=(c,d)\). If \(\beta<d\), then \({\lvert \bs\phi(t)\rvert }\to\infty\) as \(t\to\beta\).

Proof. Since \(\beta< d\), I can use \([t_0,\beta]\times\{{\lvert \bs x\rvert }\leq R\}\subseteq U\) as a compact set. Theorem 2.48 implies that \({\lvert \bs \phi(t)\rvert }>R\) for \(t\) close to \(\beta\). Since \(R\) is arbitrary, the conclusion follows. ■

I will postpone the discussion of another very useful corollary to the next section, and conclude this section with the so-called comparison theorem, which is often useful in deciding how far solutions to scalar equations can be continued.

Theorem 2.51 (Comparison theorem). Consider the ODE \(\dot y=g(t,y),\,y(t_0)=x_0\), \(y(t)\in \R\), and assume that \(g\) is Lipschitz in \(y\) for \(t\geq t_0\). Assume that \(x\) with \(x(t_0)=x_0\) satisfies the differential inequality \(\dot x\leq g(t,x)\) for \(t\geq t_0\). Then \(x(t)\leq y(t)\) for \(t\geq t_0\) where both \(x\) and \(y\) are defined.

Proof. Looking for a contradiction, assume that there is \(t_2> t_0\) such that \(x(t_2)>y(t_2)\). This implies that there is the largest \(t_1\in[t_0,t_2]\) such that \(x(t_1)\leq y(t_1)\). Moreover, \(x(t_1)=y(t_1)\) by continuity. Take \[ z(t)=x(t)-y(t)\geq 0,\quad t\in[t_1,t_2]. \] I have (since \(g\) is Lipschitz) \[ \dot z(t)=\dot x(t)-\dot y(t)\leq g(t,x(t))-g(t,y(t))\leq L(x(t)-y(t))=Lz(t). \] The last differential inequality implies (see the exercise) that \[ z(t)\leq z(t_1)e^{L(t-t_1)}=0 \] or \(x(t)\leq y(t)\). Contradiction. ■
