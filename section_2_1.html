Dynamical systems, phase flows, and differential equations

Chapter 2 · Fundamental theorems

A dynamical system is a mathematical formalization of an evolutionary deterministic process. An evolutionary (i.e., changing with time) process is called deterministic if, given the initial state, the past and future of the process can be inferred. Sometimes semi-deterministic processes are considered, when the initial condition determines only the future, but not the past. The evolutionary processes I will consider in these notes will always be deterministic.

For example, consider a pendulum. It is known from physics that the knowledge of the initial angle \(\theta\) and the initial angular velocity \(\dot \theta\) uniquely defines both the position and velocity of the pendulum in the past and in the future. The pair of variables \((\theta,\dot \theta)\) is called a phase point belonging to the phase or state space \(X\), which in this particular example can be taken as the cylinder \(\mathbf S^1\times \R\) because I need to know the angle \(\theta\) only modulo \(2\pi\) (\(\mathbf S^1\) denotes the unit circle).

If I take, e.g., an ecological system then sometimes it is reasonable to assume that the phase point is just one number \(n\in\R_+\coloneqq\{n\in \R\colon n\geq 0\}\), if the future and the past population sizes can be determined by its current population size \(n_0\).

The mentioned two examples (as well as many others) lead to the conclusion that the mathematical formalization of an evolutionary deterministic process should include three ingredients: the ordered time set \(T\) (I need to be able to talk about “future” and, sometimes, “past”, hence \(T\) must have a direction), the state space \(X\), and the law that actually determines the evolution, or, more precisely, the one-parameter family of operators \(\{\varphi^t\}\) acting on the state space \(X\), \(\varphi^t\colon X\longrightarrow X\) for each fixed \(t\in T\). To make sure that the one-parameter family of evolutionary operators corresponds to our intuitive understanding of the deterministic process, it is necessary to demand that1 \begin{equation} \varphi^0 x=x,\quad x\in X, \tag{2.1}\end{equation} i.e., that \(\varphi^0\) is the identity operator on \(X\). This means that the dynamical system does not change its state “spontaneously.” Second, it is natural to require that \begin{equation} \varphi^{\tau+t}x=\varphi^{\tau}\varphi^{t}x=\varphi^t\varphi^\tau x,\quad x\in X,\quad \tau,t,\tau+t\in T, \tag{2.2}\end{equation} i.e., that it is the same to evolve the system for \(t+\tau\) time units, or first evolve it for \(t\) time units, and then for \(\tau\) time units. Properties (2.1), (2.2) mean that the one-parameter family of evolutionary operators \(\{\varphi^t\}\) forms a transformation group (actually, an abelian group by (2.2)) acting on the state space \(X\). Indeed, from (2.2) it follows that for each \(t\in T\) the unique inverse element of \(\varphi^t\) is \(\varphi^{-t}\) since \[ \varphi^{t}\circ\varphi^{-t}=\varphi^{0}, \] assuming that \(-t\in T\).

Definition 2.1. A dynamical system is a triple \(\{T,X,\varphi^t\}\), where \(T\subseteq \R\) is a time set containing \(0\) and closed under addition, \(X\) is the state space, and \(\varphi^t\colon X\longrightarrow X,\,t\in T\), is a one-parameter family of evolutionary operators that satisfy (2.1) and (2.2).

I emphasize that this definition gives a rigorous mathematical description of what we intuitively call a deterministic system; however, in order to apply it to real situations it is necessary to have at hand the information on \(T\), \(X\), and, most importantly, on \(\{\varphi^t\}\). Unfortunately, the cases when we know the family \(\{\varphi^t\}\) explicitly are quite rare.

The set \(T\) can usually be taken as \(\Z\) or \(\R\) (or \(\Z_+\) and \(\R_+\) for semi-deterministic systems, which are also called non-invertible dynamical systems). If \(T=\Z\) or \(T=\Z_+\) one speaks of a discrete dynamical system, if \(T=\R\) — of a continuous dynamical system. For continuous dynamical systems the family \(\{\varphi^t\}\) is often called the flow or phase flow of the dynamical system.

In this course I will assume that \(X\) is an open subset of \(\R^k\), unless stated otherwise (e.g., for the pendulum above, where \(X=\mathbf S^1\times\R\)). This means that any state of my system can be completely described by \(k\) numbers, and hence the dynamical systems I will consider are finite-dimensional.

Before turning to continuous dynamical systems, consider a prototypical example of a non-invertible discrete dynamical system. In this case the family of evolutionary operators \(\{\varphi^t\}\) forms a semigroup (elements of the set need not have inverses).

Example 2.2. Consider \(T=\Z_+=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\), \(X=[0,1]\), and \[ \varphi^1\colon x\mapsto \lambda x(1-x),\quad 0\leq \lambda\leq 4. \] This is a discrete dynamical system, which is defined in terms of the single operator \(\varphi^1\). Knowledge of this operator allows me to get \(\varphi^n\) for any \(n\in \Z_+\) as the \(n\)-fold composition of \(\varphi^1\): \[ \varphi^n=\varphi^1\circ\ldots\circ \varphi^1. \] Obviously, (2.1) and (2.2) are satisfied. Note that the map \(\varphi^1\) is non-invertible, and therefore I can only predict the future, not the past. I will not discuss discrete dynamical systems in this course; I just mention that they turn out to be very important for understanding complicated behavior of systems of ODE studied in more advanced courses.

Now consider the simplest example of a continuous dynamical system.

Example 2.3. Let \(T=\R\), \(X=\R\), \(\varphi^t \colon x\mapsto e^{a t}x\) for some fixed \(a\in \R\). Check that properties (2.1) and (2.2) are satisfied. Note that the evolutionary operator here solves the scalar ODE \(\dot x=ax\).

While considering continuous dynamical systems I usually require that \(\varphi\colon T\times X\longrightarrow X\) be continuously differentiable with respect to both \(t\) and \(x\). Since the inverse for \(\varphi^{t}\) is given by \(\varphi^{-t}\) and therefore also differentiable, \(\{\varphi^t\}\) is a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms2. So if \(\varphi\in\mathcal C^{(1)}\) then I speak of a smooth or differentiable dynamical system or smooth or differentiable flow (in the latter case “smooth” and “differentiable” are usually omitted). Note that there is no universally accepted terminology; in these notes “smooth” always means \(\mathcal C^{(1)}\), unless stated otherwise.

For a smooth finite-dimensional dynamical system the vector of the phase velocity is defined at each point \(\bs x\in X\): \begin{equation} \left.\frac{\D}{\D t}\varphi^t\bs x\right|_{t=0}=\bs f(\bs x), \tag{2.3}\end{equation} and the vector \(\bs f\) of the phase velocity defines a vector field everywhere in \(X\).

Let \(X\subseteq \R^k\), \(\bs x_0\in X\) and consider the evolution of \(\bs x_0\) under the action of the flow \(\{\varphi^t\}\) as a function of \(t\). In other words, I consider the mapping \[ \bs \phi\colon \R\longrightarrow X,\quad \bs \phi(t)=\bs \phi(t;\bs x_0)\coloneqq\varphi^t\bs x_0. \]

Theorem 2.4. The mapping \(\bs \phi\) solves the IVP \[ \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(\bs x),\quad \bs x(0)=\bs x_0, \] where the vector field on the right-hand side is defined by (2.3).

Proof. This follows from (2.1), since \(\bs\phi(0)=\varphi^0\bs x_0=\bs x_0\) and (2.2): \[ \frac{\D\bs \phi}{\D \tau}(\tau)=\left.\frac{\D}{\D t}\varphi^t \bs x_0\right|_{t=\tau}=\left.\frac{\D}{\D \epsilon}\varphi^{\tau+\epsilon} \bs x_0\right|_{\epsilon=0}=\left.\frac{\D}{\D \epsilon}\varphi^{\epsilon}\varphi^\tau \bs x_0\right|_{\epsilon=0}=\bs f(\varphi^\tau\bs x_0)=\bs f(\bs \phi(\tau)). \] (If you are confused by one of the steps, convince yourself that it is just \(g'(\epsilon+\tau)|_{\epsilon=0}=g'(t)|_{t=\tau}\) in disguise for differentiable \(g\).) ■

In words, for each smooth dynamical system defined on a finite-dimensional state space \(X\subseteq \R^k\) I found a system of autonomous ordinary differential equations defined by the vector field \(\bs f\) of the phase velocity (sometimes this system of ODE or, equivalently, the vector field \(\bs f\), is called the generator of the flow \(\varphi^t\)). Solutions to this system are the images of the movement of the phase points under the action of the phase flow. Therefore, my attempt to give a mathematical formalization for evolutionary deterministic processes showed that it is not necessary to formulate the deterministic laws of nature in terms of the transformation group \(\{\varphi^t\}\) acting on the state space \(X\); it is also possible to do so in terms of the corresponding vector field \(\bs f\) or, equivalently, in terms of an autonomous system of ODE. The latter approach reduces any problem about the evolution of the process to a geometric problem about the behavior of the flow of the given vector field.

Example 2.5. Returning to the example of the pendulum, Newton’s second law leads to the second-order differential equation (if I disregard the friction) \[ \ddot \theta+\frac{g}{l}\sin \theta=0. \] This equation is equivalent to the following autonomous system of two first-order ODE: \begin{align*} \dot x_1&=x_2,\\ \dot x_2&=-\frac{g}{l}\sin x_1, \end{align*} where \((x_1,x_2)\coloneqq(\theta,\dot \theta)\in X=\mathbf S^1\times \R\). The vector field of this system defines the corresponding flow \(\{\varphi^t\}\). I will not be able to write \(\varphi\) down explicitly in elementary functions, but this specific system of ODE will allow me to study many of its properties!

The main question is whether the converse is true. That is, does an arbitrary smooth vector field (an autonomous system of ODE) generate a smooth dynamical system? A complete answer to this question requires a careful analysis of the properties of the solutions to the general ODE system, and this will be my task for the next several lectures.

  1. Since my evolutionary operator is acting on an abstract space \(X\), I abuse the notation by writing \(\varphi^t x\) to denote \(\varphi^t(x)\).↩︎

  2. Recall that a map \(g\colon U\longrightarrow V\) is a diffeomorphism if \(g\in\mathcal C^{(1)}(U;V)\), has an inverse, and the inverse is a \(\mathcal C^{(1)}(V;U)\) map.↩︎
