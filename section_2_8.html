2.8.1Inverse and implicit function theorems

Several times in this course I refer to either inverse or implicit function theorems. In this appendix I state these theorems and also show how they are connected to the theory of ODE.

Consider the following system of \(n\) nonlinear equations with \(n\) unknowns: \[ \bs x=\bs f(\bs y),\quad \bs x,\bs y\in\R^n, \] and assume that \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(1)}\) in some neighborhood of the point \(\bs a\in\R^n\), \(\bs b=\bs f(\bs a)\). The inverse function theorem deals with the situation when the unknown \(\bs y\) can be expressed locally as a function of \(\bs x\): \[ \bs y=\bs g(\bs x),\quad \bs a=\bs g(\bs b). \] The function \(\bs g\) is called the inverse function to \(\bs f\), and the existence of such function is equivalent to saying that \(\bs f\) is a bijection (one-to-one and onto) from some neighborhood \(U\) of \(\bs a\) to some neighborhood \(W\) of \(\bs b\).

Heuristically, using Taylor’s formula for \(\bs f\): \[ \bs f(\bs y)=\bs f(\bs a)+\bs f'(\bs a)(\bs y-\bs a)+o({\lvert \bs y-\bs a\rvert }), \] where \[ \bs f'(\bs a)=\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial y_1}(\bs a) & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial y_n}(\bs a) \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial y_1}(\bs a) & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial y_n}(\bs a) \\ \end{bmatrix} \] is the Jacobi matrix of \(\bs f\) evaluated at \(\bs a\). Dropping the term \(o({\lvert \bs y-\bs a\rvert })\), I get \[ \bs x-\bs b=\bs f'(\bs a)(\bs y-\bs a), \] which is a linear system and therefore has a unique solution if and only if \(\det \bs f'(\bs a)\neq 0\). It turns out that exactly the same condition is sufficient for \(\bs f\) to be a bijection in \(U\).

Theorem 2.61. Let \(\bs a,\bs b\in \R^n\) and \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(1)}\) in some neighborhood of \(\bs a\), \(\bs f(\bs a)=\bs b\). Assume that \(\det \bs f'(\bs a)\neq 0\). Then there exist open \(U,W\subseteq \R^n\), \(\bs a\in U,\,\bs b\in W\), such that

\((a)\) \(\bs f\) is a bijection from \(U\) to \(W\);

\((b)\) The inverse function \(\bs g\in\mathcal C^{(1)}(W;U)\), and \(\bs g'(\bs b)=\bs f'(\bs a)^{-1}\).

Exercise 2.33. Prove part \((a)\) of Theorem 2.61. (Hint: Assume without loss of generality that \(\bs a=\bs b=0\), let \(B=\bs f'(0)\), and let \(\bs h(\bs y)=\bs f(\bs y)-B\bs y\), so that the equation \(\bs x=\bs f(\bs y)\) is equivalent to \(\bs y=T\bs y\), where \(T\colon \bs y\mapsto B^{-1}(\bs x-\bs h(\bs y))\). Since \(\bs h'(\bs y)=\bs f'(\bs y)-B\) is continuous and \(\bs h'(0)=0\), there is \(r>0\) such that \({\lvert B^{-1}(\bs h(\bs y_1)-\bs h(\bs y_2))\rvert }\leq\frac12{\lvert \bs y_1-\bs y_2\rvert }\) for \({\lvert \bs y_1\rvert },{\lvert \bs y_2\rvert }\leq r\) (compare with the proof of Lemma 2.39). Show that if \({\lvert B^{-1}\bs x\rvert }\leq r/2\), then \(T\) maps the closed ball \(\{{\lvert \bs y\rvert }\leq r\}\) into itself, and apply Theorem 2.34.)

Now consider the system \[ \bs F(\bs x,\bs y)=0,\quad \bs F\colon \R^{n+m}\longrightarrow \R^m,\, \bs x\in\R^n,\,\bs y\in \R^m. \] I need to identify the conditions under which the variables \(\bs y\) can be uniquely expressed through \(\bs x\): \[ \bs y=\bs g(\bs x), \] or \[ \bs F(\bs x,\bs g(\bs x))=0. \]

Theorem 2.62. Assume that \(\bs a\in\R^n,\,\bs b\in \R^m\) and \(\bs F(\bs a,\bs b)=0\). Assume that \(\bs F\in\mathcal C^{(1)}\) in some neighborhood of the point \((\bs a,\bs b)\) and \(\det \bs F_{\bs y}(\bs a,\bs b)\neq 0 \), where \(\bs F_{\bs y}(\bs a,\bs b)\) is the Jacobi matrix of \(\bs F\) with respect to \(\bs y\). Then there exist open \(U\subseteq \R^n\), \(W\subseteq \R^m\), \(\bs a\in U,\,\bs b\in W\), such that

\((a)\) There exists a unique \(\bs g\colon U\longrightarrow W\), which satisfies \[ \bs F(\bs x,\bs g(\bs x))\equiv 0,\quad \bs x\in U; \]

\((b)\) \(\bs g\in\mathcal C^{(1)}(U;W)\).

Exercise 2.34. Prove Theorem 2.62. (Hint: Apply Theorem 2.61 to the map \((\bs x,\bs y)\mapsto(\bs x,\bs F(\bs x,\bs y))\) from \(\R^{n+m}\) to itself; its Jacobi matrix is block triangular with determinant \(\det\bs F_{\bs y}\).)

Exercise 2.35. Prove Theorem 2.61 from Theorem 2.62.

The stated theorems and exercises show that implicit and inverse function theorems are equivalent, and their proof can be performed by the same device that I used to prove the existence and uniqueness theorem for ODE. These facts imply that there is a strong connection between these theorems and the theory of differential equations1. In particular, I can even prove (a version of) the implicit function theorem using my main result on ODE.

Theorem 2.63. Let \(U\subseteq \R^{k+1}\) be open and \(\bs F\in\mathcal C^{(2)}(U;\R^k)\). Assume that \(\bs F(t_0,\bs x_0)=0\), \((t_0,\bs x_0)\in U\), and the Jacobi matrix \(\bs F_{\bs x}(t_0,\bs x_0)\) is nonsingular. Then there exist an open interval \(I\coloneqq(t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta)\) and \(\bs\phi\in \mathcal C^{(1)}(I;\R^k)\) such that \(\bs \phi(t_0)=\bs x_0\) and \[ \bs F(t,\bs \phi(t))\equiv 0,\quad t\in I. \]

Proof. Since \(\det\bs F_{\bs x}\) is continuous, there exists an open neighborhood \(U_0\subseteq U\) of \((t_0,\bs x_0)\) in which \(\bs F_{\bs x}\) is nonsingular. Hence I can define \[ \bs f(t,\bs x)=-\bs F_{\bs x}(t,\bs x)^{-1}\bs F_t(t,\bs x),\quad (t,\bs x)\in U_0. \] The entries of the inverse matrix are rational functions of the entries of \(\bs F_{\bs x}\) (Cramer’s rule), and since \(\bs F\in\mathcal C^{(2)}\), I get \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(1)}(U_0;\R^k)\); hence \(\bs f\) is locally Lipschitz in \(\bs x\) (Lemma 2.39). Consider the ODE \[ \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x),\quad \bs x(t_0)=\bs x_0. \] By Theorem 2.42, there exists a unique solution \(\bs\phi\) to this problem on some interval \(I=(t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta)\), whose graph lies in \(U_0\). Note that \(\bs F(t_0,\bs\phi(t_0))=\bs F(t_0,\bs x_0)=0\) and, by the chain rule, \[ \frac{\D}{\D t}\bs F(t,\bs\phi(t))=\bs F_t(t,\bs\phi(t))+\bs F_{\bs x}(t,\bs\phi(t))\,\dot{\bs\phi}(t)=\bs F_t-\bs F_{\bs x}\bs F_{\bs x}^{-1}\bs F_t=0, \] therefore \[ \bs F(t,\bs\phi(t))\equiv 0,\quad t\in I. \] ■

Remark 2.64. The assumption \(\bs F\in\mathcal C^{(2)}\) is used only to make the right-hand side \(\bs f\) continuously differentiable, and hence locally Lipschitz. Actually, it can be shown that \(\bs F\in\mathcal C^{(1)}(U;\R^k)\) is enough to prove the theorem.

Remark 2.65. I showed that the implicit function theorem is a consequence of the existence and uniqueness theorem for ODE. The converse is also true, but I will postpone the discussion of this fact until the end of the next chapter.
