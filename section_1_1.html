Newton’s second law, ordinary differential equations, and the three-body problem

Chapter 1 · Introduction

By Newton’s second law the product of the mass1 of a body and its acceleration is equal to the net force applied to this body: \[ m\bs a=\bs F, \] where \(m\) is a scalar, mass of the body, \(\bs a(t)\in\R^3\) is a vector with three components at each time moment \(t\), \(\bs a\colon \R\longrightarrow \R^3\), and \(\bs F\bigl(t,\bs x(t),\bs{v}(t)\bigr)\in \R^3\) is the net force, which may depend in general on the time \(t\), current displacement \(\bs x(t)\in \R^3\), or current velocity \(\bs v(t)\in \R^3\) of the body.

First let me assume for simplicity that the body under question has only one degree of freedom, which means that one coordinate is enough to specify the body’s position. In this case Newton’s second law looks simpler: \[ ma=F, \] where now \(a(t)\) and \(F(t,x(t),v(t))\) are scalars at the fixed moment \(t\). Since acceleration \(a\) is the rate of change of velocity \(v\), and velocity is the rate of change of displacement \(x\), \[ a=\frac{\D v}{\D t}\,,\quad v=\frac{\D x}{\D t}\,, \] and hence the physical law takes the form \[ m\frac{\D^2 x}{\D t^2}=F, \] or \begin{equation} m\ddot x=F, \tag{1.1}\end{equation} using the notation due to Isaac Newton himself: \[ \ddot x\coloneqq\frac{\D^2 x}{\D t^2}\,,\quad \dot x\coloneqq\frac{\D x}{\D t}\,. \] Equation (1.1) is a basic example of an ordinary differential equation, in which our goal is to find the function \(t\mapsto x(t)\) that has two continuous derivatives and satisfies the equation.

Example 1.1 (No force). Assume that \(F=0\), then \(\ddot x=0\), and I can directly integrate (Newton invented Calculus to solve differential equations!) twice with respect to \(t\) to find the solution \[ x(t)= C_1 t+C_2, \] where \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are arbitrary (real, if we stick to the real-valued solutions) constants. To determine these constants I need the initial conditions that can be given, e.g., as \(x(0)=x_0\) (initial position) and \(\dot x(0)=v_0\) (initial velocity). This implies that my solution is the function \[ x(t)= x_0+v_0t, \] which is actually a mathematical manifestation of Newton’s first law, which states that “an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.”

Example 1.2 (Constant force). Now let \(F=\text{const}\). Directing the \(x\)-axis upward and writing \(F=-mg\) (the minus sign is chosen by tradition, so that a force pointing down corresponds to \(g>0\)), I get \[ \ddot x=-g,\quad g=\text{const}\in \R. \] I again can simply integrate twice to find, using the same initial conditions as in the previous example, that \[ x(t)= x_0+v_0t-\frac{gt^2}{2} \] is my solution. For example, the fate of a stone thrown vertically down (\(v_0<0\)) or up (\(v_0>0\)) from some height \(x_0\) above the Earth’s ground is governed, approximately, by this solution.

Example 1.3 (Linear force). Now let \(F\propto x\), where \(\propto\) means “proportional.” For example, Hooke’s law states that “the force needed to extend or compress a spring by some distance is proportional to that distance.” Hence I have \[ m\ddot x=-kx,\quad k>0, \] where \(k\) is called the spring constant. I can rewrite this differential equation as \[ \ddot x+\omega^2x=0,\quad \omega^2\coloneqq\frac{k}{m}\,. \] This ordinary differential equation (ODE from now on) cannot be solved by simple consecutive integrations, but one can check that both \(x_1(t)= \sin \omega t\) and \(x_2(t)= \cos \omega t\) solve this equation, as well as any linear combination of them \[ x(t)= C_1x_1(t)+C_2x_2(t)=C_1\sin \omega t+C_2\cos \omega t. \] Actually the last solution, as I will prove in this course, is the general solution to \(\ddot x+\omega^2x=0\), i.e., any solution to this equation can be expressed with this formula for some specific values of \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).

Can every ODE be solved analytically? Not really.

Example 1.4 (Three-body problem). According to Newton’s law of gravity, two bodies of masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) respectively are attracted to each other with the force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Hence, if I have three bodies, and the only forces that are applied to them are gravitational, then Newton’s second law implies for the first body that \[ m_1\ddot{\bs x}_1=G\frac{m_1m_2}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert }^2}\cdot\frac{\bs x_2-\bs x_1}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert }}+G\frac{m_1m_3}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_3\rvert }^2}\cdot\frac{\bs x_3-\bs x_1}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_3\rvert }}\,, \] where \(G\) is the constant of proportionality in Newton’s law of gravity, \({\lvert \bs x\rvert }\coloneqq\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2}\) for the vector2 \(\bs x^\top=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\in \R^3\), such that \({\lvert \bs x-\bs y\rvert }\) is the usual Euclidean distance between points \(\bs x,\bs y\in \R^3\), and \((\bs x_2-\bs x_1)/{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert }\) is the unit vector in the direction from the first body to the second one. I will also need two more equations for the second and third bodies: \begin{align*} m_2\ddot{\bs x}_2&=G\frac{m_1m_2}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert }^2}\cdot\frac{\bs x_1-\bs x_2}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert }}+G\frac{m_2m_3}{{\lvert \bs x_2-\bs x_3\rvert }^2}\cdot\frac{\bs x_3-\bs x_2}{{\lvert \bs x_2-\bs x_3\rvert }}\,,\\ m_3\ddot{\bs x}_3&=G\frac{m_2m_3}{{\lvert \bs x_2-\bs x_3\rvert }^2}\cdot\frac{\bs x_2-\bs x_3}{{\lvert \bs x_2-\bs x_3\rvert }}+G\frac{m_1m_3}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_3\rvert }^2}\cdot\frac{\bs x_1-\bs x_3}{{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_3\rvert }}\,. \end{align*} Since I deal with the three-dimensional Euclidean space, I have a total of 9 equations, and I need 18 initial conditions. It turns out that we still lack a full understanding of the behavior of solutions to this system of ODEs. Here is a short digest of the history of this problem3.

1687

Isaac Newton in his Principia (Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica) was the first one to formulate the problem of determining the positions of three massive bodies (the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon).

1747

Jean d’Alembert and Alexis Clairaut published the first analytical treatment of the three-body problem.

1760

Leonhard Euler solved exactly a related problem: the motion of a body attracted by two fixed gravitational centers.

1767–1772

Euler found the periodic solutions of the three-body problem in which the three bodies always stay on one line (1767), and Joseph-Louis Lagrange found the periodic solutions in which they stay at the vertices of an equilateral triangle (1772). These remain the only known solutions of the general three-body problem in closed form.

1887

Ernst Bruns proved that the three-body problem cannot be solved analytically (well, my statement is oversimplification here, look it up).

1889

Henri Poincaré won a contest on the best solution to the three-body problem. He did not solve, of course, this problem, but formulated the beginning of the theory that eventually led to what is often called the chaos theory nowadays.

1912

Karl Sundman constructed a power series solution, which means that, in a sense, we do have an analytical solution to the three-body problem in the form of an infinite series. The usefulness of this solution, however, is doubtful at best: the series converges extremely slowly.

1975

Roger Broucke and Michel Hénon (and, independently, John Hadjidemetriou) found a new family of periodic solutions.

1993–2000

Cristopher Moore numerically discovered the “figure-eight” periodic solution, in which three equal masses chase each other along a single figure-eight-shaped curve (1993); Alain Chenciner and Richard Montgomery gave a rigorous proof of its existence (2000).

2013

By means of elaborate numerical calculations, 13 new periodic solutions belonging to 11 new families were found4.

2020

Neural networks were used to accurately integrate differential equations for the three-body problem5.

2024

Discovery of “islands of regularity” in the chaos6.

To conclude, the history of attempts to analyze the three-body problem is definitely not over yet (one thing to note though is that the vast majority of the latest achievements are connected to implementing more and more precise numerical methods, not to analysis of the problem per se).

Exercise 1.1. The exact dynamics of a stone dropped from height \(h\) above the Earth, if I disregard the air resistance, is described by Newton’s second law combined with Newton’s law of gravity: \[ m\ddot x=-G\frac{mM}{{\lvert x+R\rvert }^2}\,, \] where \(x\) is the height of the stone above the Earth’s surface, \(m\) is the mass of the stone, \(M\) is the mass of the Earth, and \(R\) is the radius of the Earth. If I assume that \(x+R\approx R\), then I get an approximate equation, whose solution I denote by \(y\): \[ \ddot y=-g,\quad g\coloneqq G\frac{M}{R^2}\,. \] Assuming that \(x(0)=y(0)=h>0\) and \(\dot x(0)=\dot y(0)=0\), for which equation is the time to reach the surface of the Earth smaller? Please note that you do not need to actually solve the equations.
