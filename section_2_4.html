2.4.1Proof for a system of first-order ODE

In this section I will use Banach’s fixed point theorem to give a relatively short proof of the existence and uniqueness theorem for systems of first-order ODE. Conceptually, Banach’s theorem is an abstract version of Picard’s original method of successive approximations. Before getting to the actual theorem and its proof I still need to discuss certain technical points, the most important of which is the required regularity of my \(\bs f\).

I aim to prove that the problem \begin{equation} \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x),\quad \bs x(t)\in X\subseteq \R^k,\quad \bs f\colon J\times X\longrightarrow \R^k,\quad \bs x(t_0)=\bs x_0\in X, \tag{2.7}\end{equation} where \(J\subseteq \R\) is an interval, has a unique solution.

In the following I will use two norms. First, for this section for any element \(\bs x\) of \(\R^k\) I fix the norm \[ {\lvert \bs x\rvert }=\max_{i}{\lvert x_i\rvert }, \] note the absolute value notation for this norm. Second, for a continuous vector function \(\bs x\colon I\longrightarrow \R^k\) I use the uniform norm \[ {\lVert \bs x\rVert }_\infty=\max_{t\in I}{\lvert \bs x(t)\rvert }, \] where the absolute value is exactly the previous norm. Here \(I\) is a closed and bounded interval. Recall from the last section that the normed vector space \((\mathcal C(I;\R^k),{\lVert \cdot\rVert }_\infty)\) is a Banach space (Corollary 2.30).

I will also need

Lemma 2.36. If \(\bs x\in \mathcal C(I;\R^k)\) and \(t_1,t_2\in I\) then \[ {\left\lvert \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \bs x(t)\D t\right\rvert }\leq {\left\lvert \int_{t_1}^{t_2}{\lvert \bs x(t)\rvert }\D t\right\rvert }. \]

Proof. I have \[ {\left\lvert \int_{t_1}^{t_2} x_j(t)\D t\right\rvert }\leq {\left\lvert \int_{t_1}^{t_2} {\lvert x_j(t)\rvert }\D t\right\rvert }\leq {\left\lvert \int_{t_1}^{t_2} {\lvert \bs x(t)\rvert }\D t\right\rvert } \] since \[ {\lvert x_j(t)\rvert }\leq \max_{i}{\lvert x_i(t)\rvert }={\lvert \bs x(t)\rvert }. \] The right-hand side does not depend on \(j\). Taking the maximum over \(j\) on the left-hand side, I get the needed inequality. ■

Recall from the first chapter that in a number of cases assuming that the right-hand side of an ODE is \(\mathcal C^{(1)}\) is sufficient to guarantee existence and uniqueness of solutions. Here I will use a similar regularity condition on \(\bs f\) that will be sufficient for both existence and uniqueness.

Definition 2.37. Let \(U\subseteq \R\times \R^k\). A function \(\bs f\colon U\longrightarrow \R^k\) is called Lipschitz continuous with respect to \(\bs x\) (or Lipschitz in \(\bs x\)) in \(U\) if there exists a constant \(L\geq 0\) such that \[ {\lvert \bs f(t,\bs x_1)-\bs f(t,\bs x_2)\rvert }\leq L{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert } \] for any points \((t,\bs x_1),(t,\bs x_2)\in U\). The constant \(L\) is independent of the points \((t,\bs x_1)\) and \((t,\bs x_2)\) and is called the Lipschitz constant.

Note that only points with the same \(t\) are compared: no condition is imposed on the dependence of \(\bs f\) on \(t\) (if \(\bs f\) does not depend on \(t\), I will simply speak of Lipschitz functions). Note also that, since all norms on \(\R^k\) are equivalent (Theorem 2.29), whether \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz in \(\bs x\) does not depend on the choice of the norm; only the value of \(L\) does.

The requirement that \(\bs f\) be Lipschitz in \(\bs x\) in the whole domain \(U\) is often too restrictive. A more general notion is given by the following definition.

Definition 2.38. Let \(U\subseteq \R\times \R^k\) be open. A function \(\bs f\colon U\longrightarrow \R^k\) is called locally Lipschitz continuous with respect to \(\bs x\) in \(U\) if every point \((t_0,\bs x_0)\in U\) has a neighborhood \(U_0\subseteq U\) in which \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz in \(\bs x\). The Lipschitz constant may depend on \(U_0\).

There is a simple criterion to determine whether a given \(\bs f\) is locally Lipschitz.

Lemma 2.39. Let \(U\subseteq \R\times\R^k\) be open, and let \(\bs f=(f_1,\ldots,f_k)^\top\colon U\longrightarrow\R^k\) be such that all the partial derivatives \(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\), \(i,j=1,\ldots,k\), exist and are continuous in \(U\). Then \(\bs f\) is locally Lipschitz in \(\bs x\) in \(U\).

Proof. Fix \((t_0,\bs x_0)\in U\). Since \(U\) is open, there exist \(a,b>0\) such that the cube \[ P=\left\{(t,\bs x)\colon {\lvert t-t_0\rvert }\leq a,\ {\lvert \bs x-\bs x_0\rvert }\leq b\right\} \] lies in \(U\) (recall that \({\lvert \bs x\rvert }=\max_i{\lvert x_i\rvert }\), so \(P\) is indeed a cube). The set \(P\) is compact, and therefore the continuous functions \(\partial f_i/\partial x_j\) are bounded on \(P\): \[ m=\max_{i,j}\max_{P}{\left\lvert \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}\right\rvert }<\infty. \] Take \((t,\bs x),(t,\bs y)\in P\) and consider the points \[ \bs u^{(j)}=(x_1,\ldots,x_j,y_{j+1},\ldots,y_k)^\top,\quad j=0,\ldots,k, \] so that \(\bs u^{(0)}=\bs y\) and \(\bs u^{(k)}=\bs x\). Each coordinate of \(\bs u^{(j)}\) coincides with the corresponding coordinate of either \(\bs x\) or \(\bs y\), hence \((t,\bs u^{(j)})\in P\), and the same is true for all the points of the segment connecting \(\bs u^{(j-1)}\) and \(\bs u^{(j)}\) (here I use that \(P\) is a cube). The points \(\bs u^{(j-1)}\) and \(\bs u^{(j)}\) differ only in the \(j\)-th coordinate, therefore, by the mean value theorem applied to the function of one variable \(s\mapsto f_i(t,x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1},s,y_{j+1},\ldots,y_k)\), \[ f_i(t,\bs u^{(j)})-f_i(t,\bs u^{(j-1)})=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}(t,\bs \xi^{(j)})\,(x_j-y_j) \] for some point \(\bs \xi^{(j)}\) on the segment connecting \(\bs u^{(j-1)}\) and \(\bs u^{(j)}\). Summing over \(j\), I get \[ {\lvert f_i(t,\bs x)-f_i(t,\bs y)\rvert }\leq \sum_{j=1}^k{\lvert f_i(t,\bs u^{(j)})-f_i(t,\bs u^{(j-1)})\rvert }\leq m\sum_{j=1}^k{\lvert x_j-y_j\rvert }\leq km{\lvert \bs x-\bs y\rvert }. \] Taking the maximum over \(i\) on the left-hand side yields \[ {\lvert \bs f(t,\bs x)-\bs f(t,\bs y)\rvert }\leq km{\lvert \bs x-\bs y\rvert },\quad (t,\bs x),(t,\bs y)\in P, \] i.e., \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz in \(\bs x\) in \(P\), and hence in the interior of \(P\), which is a neighborhood of \((t_0,\bs x_0)\). ■

I will also need the following fact.

Lemma 2.40. Let \(U\subseteq \R\times\R^k\) be open and \(\bs f\in\mathcal C(U;\R^k)\). Then \(\bs f\) is locally Lipschitz in \(\bs x\) in \(U\) if and only if for every compact \(K\subseteq U\) there exists \(L_K\geq 0\) such that \[ {\lvert \bs f(t,\bs x_1)-\bs f(t,\bs x_2)\rvert }\leq L_K{\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert } \] for any \((t,\bs x_1),(t,\bs x_2)\in K\).

Exercise 2.12. Prove Lemma 2.40. (Hint: For one direction, note that since \(U\) is open, every point of \(U\) is the center of a small closed cube that lies in \(U\) and is compact. For the other one, assume the contrary: there exist \((t_n,\bs x_n),(t_n,\bs y_n)\in K\) with \({\lvert \bs f(t_n,\bs x_n)-\bs f(t_n,\bs y_n)\rvert }>n{\lvert \bs x_n-\bs y_n\rvert }\). Use the boundedness of \(\bs f\) on \(K\) to show that \({\lvert \bs x_n-\bs y_n\rvert }\to 0\), pass to a convergent subsequence, and get a contradiction with the Lipschitz condition in a neighborhood of the limit point.)

Example 2.41. Let me give a few simple examples to connect the notion of a Lipschitz function with the examples from the first chapter.

\((a)\) Let \(f(x)=x\), which is obviously Lipschitz on \(\R\) with \(L=1\). The ODE \(\dot x=f(x)\) with \(x(t_0)=x_0\) has the solution \(x(t)=x_0e^{t-t_0}\) defined on all \(\R\).

\((b)\) Let \(f(x)=x^2\). It is continuously differentiable everywhere, and hence locally Lipschitz. Indeed, for any interval \([a,b]\), the constant \(L_{[a,b]}=2\max\{{\lvert a\rvert },{\lvert b\rvert }\}\) will work. However, this function is not Lipschitz on \(\R\), since \({\lvert x^2-y^2\rvert }={\lvert x+y\rvert }{\lvert x-y\rvert }\), and for any constant \(L\geq 0\) I can find \(x,y\) such that \({\lvert x+y\rvert }>L\). Recall Example 1.20, where I showed that the solutions to \(\dot x=x^2\), although they exist and are unique for any point \((t_0,x_0)\), are local and cannot, in general, be extended to all of \(\R\).

\((c)\) Let \(f(x)=x^{1/3}\) on \([-1,1]\). This function is not differentiable at \(0\). It is not Lipschitz either. Indeed, taking \(y=0,x>0\) and assuming that \(f\) is Lipschitz implies that \[ \frac{1}{x^{2/3}}\leq L, \] which is impossible for \(x\to 0^+\). Recall Example 1.16, where I showed that the solution to \(\dot x=x^{1/3}\) with \(x(0)=0\) is not unique.

\((d)\) Let \(f(x)={\lvert x\rvert }\). This function is not differentiable at \(0\), and yet \[ {\bigl\lvert {\lvert x\rvert }-{\lvert y\rvert }\bigr\rvert }\leq {\lvert x-y\rvert }, \] hence \(f\) is Lipschitz on \(\R\) with \(L=1\).

\((e)\) Let \(f(t,x)=\sqrt{{\lvert t\rvert }}\,x\) on \(U=(-1,1)\times\R\). This function is not differentiable with respect to \(t\) at \(t=0\), and yet it is Lipschitz in \(x\) in \(U\) with \(L=1\), since \({\lvert f(t,x)-f(t,y)\rvert }=\sqrt{{\lvert t\rvert }}{\lvert x-y\rvert }\leq{\lvert x-y\rvert }\). Only the dependence on \(x\) matters.

All the hard work has been done and finally everything is ready to prove the long-promised theorem1.

Theorem 2.42. Consider problem (2.7) and assume that \(\bs f\) is continuous and locally Lipschitz continuous in \(\bs x\) in a domain \(U\subseteq J\times X\) such that \((t_0,\bs x_0)\in U\), and \(U\) is open. Then problem (2.7) has a unique solution \(t\mapsto \bs \phi(t)\) defined on some interval \([t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta],\,\delta>0\).

Proof. Let \(P\) be the \((k+1)\)-dimensional cube \({\lvert \bs x-\bs x_0\rvert }\leq b,\,{\lvert t-t_0\rvert }\leq a\) such that \(P\subseteq U\). Denote \(M=\max_{P}{\lvert \bs f(t,\bs x)\rvert }\). As the Banach space \(Y\) I take the set of continuous vector functions \(\mathcal C([t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta];\R^k)\), where \(0<\delta\leq a\) will be determined later. Consider also the set \(\mathcal B\subseteq Y\), which is given by those \(\bs x\in Y\) that satisfy \({\lVert \bs x-\bs x_0\rVert }_\infty\leq b\). \(\mathcal B\) is chosen such that the graphs of \(\bs x\in \mathcal B\) are in \(P\). \(\mathcal B\) is a complete metric space (Corollary 2.31). Recall that problem (2.7) is equivalent (on the set of continuous functions) to the integral equation \(\bs x=T\bs x\), where the operator \(T\) is given by \[ T\colon \bs x\mapsto \bs x_0+\int_{t_0}^t\bs f(\tau,\bs x(\tau))\D\tau. \] Clearly, for \(\bs x\in\mathcal B\), \(T\bs x\) is continuous as an integral of a continuous function so that \(T\colon \mathcal B\longrightarrow Y\).

\((i)\) I will show that if \(\delta\) is small enough then \(T\colon \mathcal B\longrightarrow \mathcal B\). Note that \(\mathcal B\) was chosen such that the graph of \(\bs x\in\mathcal B\) is in \(P\). Consider for \(t\in[t_0,t_0+\delta]\) (the other side is treated similarly) \[ {\lvert (T\bs x)(t)-\bs x_0\rvert }={\left\lvert \int_{t_0}^t\bs f(\tau,\bs x(\tau))\D\tau\right\rvert }\leq {\left\lvert \int_{t_0}^t{\lvert \bs f(\tau,\bs x(\tau))\rvert }\D\tau\right\rvert }\leq {\left\lvert \int_{t_0}^t M\D\tau\right\rvert }\leq M\delta, \] therefore \[ {\lVert T\bs x-\bs x_0\rVert }_\infty\leq M\delta. \] If I choose \(\delta\) to satisfy \[ \delta\leq \frac{b}{M}\,, \] then \(T\colon \mathcal B\longrightarrow \mathcal B\).

\((ii)\) I will show that if \(\delta\) is small enough then \(T\colon \mathcal B\longrightarrow \mathcal B\) is a contraction. By Lemma 2.40, \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz continuous in \(\bs x\) in \(P\) with constant \(L_P\). Consider for \(t\in[t_0,t_0+\delta]\) (the other side is treated similarly) \begin{align*} {\lvert (T\bs x)(t)-(T\bs y)(t)\rvert }&\leq {\left\lvert \int_{t_0}^t{\lvert \bs f(\tau,\bs x(\tau))-\bs f(\tau,\bs y(\tau))\rvert }\D\tau\right\rvert }\leq L_P{\left\lvert \int_{t_0}^t{\lvert \bs x(\tau)-\bs y(\tau)\rvert }\D\tau\right\rvert }\\ &\leq L_P{\left\lvert \int_{t_0}^t{\lVert \bs x-\bs y\rVert }_\infty\D\tau\right\rvert }\leq L_P\delta {\lVert \bs x-\bs y\rVert }_\infty, \end{align*} which proves that if \[ \delta<\frac{1}{L_P}\,, \] then \(T\) is a contraction. For instance, I can take \(\delta=1/(2L_P)\) for definiteness.

Concluding, if I choose \(0<\delta=\min\{a,b/M,1/(2L_P)\}\) then \(T\) satisfies the conditions of the contraction mapping principle in \(\mathcal B\) and therefore has a unique fixed point \(\hat{\bs x}\in \mathcal B\) (if \(M=0\) or \(L_P=0\), this choice means that \(b/M\) or \(1/(2L_P)\) can be taken as \(\infty\)).

\((iii)\) The final step is to show that it is impossible to have other solutions to the stated problem on the interval \([t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta]\). Indeed, assume that \(\bs y\) is such a different solution, which is not in \(\mathcal B\). This means that the graph of \(\bs y\) leaves \(P\) when \(t\in(t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta)\). This implies that there is first \(t^\ast\) for which (it is important that for \([t_0,t^\ast]\) the graph of \(\bs y\) is still in \(P\)) \[ {\lvert \bs y(t^\ast)-\bs x_0\rvert }=b\leq M(t^\ast-t_0), \] and hence \[ t^\ast\geq t_0+\frac{b}{M}\geq t_0+\delta, \] that is, \(\bs y\) must be in \(\mathcal B\).

Hence, I found the unique continuous solution to the integral equation (2.6), and therefore, by Lemma 2.7, the unique solution to the IVP (2.7). ■

2.4.2Some remarks and extensions

Several remarks and extensions can be added to the existence and uniqueness theorem from the previous subsection.

I start with a simple corollary to the main theorem.

Corollary 2.43. Consider the IVP (2.7), and assume that \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(p)}(U;\R^k)\) with \(U\subseteq J\times X\) open, i.e., the function \(\bs f\) is \(p\geq 0\) times continuously differentiable with respect to all its arguments. Then any solution to this problem is \(p+1\) times continuously differentiable.

Exercise 2.13. Prove this corollary.

Note that in Corollary 2.43 for \(p\ge1\) the main theorem guarantees that the solution exists; for \(p=0\) existence still holds thanks to Peano’s theorem2:

Theorem 2.44 (Peano’s theorem). Consider problem (2.7) with \(U\subseteq J\times X\) open and assume that \(\bs f\in\mathcal C(U;\R^k)\). Then for each \((t_0,\bs x_0)\in U\) there exists a solution through this point.

Peano’s theorem guarantees only the existence of solutions. Uniqueness, however, may fail for \(\bs f\) just continuous (recall Example 1.16).

The non-uniqueness allowed by Peano’s theorem has a specific structure. Kneser’s theorem3 states that if \(\bs f\) is continuous, then on an interval where all the solutions through \((t_0,\bs x_0)\) exist, for each \(t\) the set of their values \(\bs x(t)\) is compact and connected (the solutions form a “funnel”). The following two exercises illustrate this statement in the scalar case.

Exercise 2.14. Consider the problem \(\dot x=x^{1/3}\), \(x(0)=0\) (Example 1.16). Describe all the solutions to this problem for \(t\geq 0\), and show that the set of their values at \(t=1\) is the interval \(\left[-\left(2/3\right)^{3/2},\left(2/3\right)^{3/2}\right]\). Sketch the funnel.

Exercise 2.15. Let \(f\) be continuous, and let \(x_1\leq x_2\) be two solutions to \(\dot x=f(t,x)\) on \([t_0,t_1]\) with \(x_1(t_0)=x_2(t_0)=x_0\). Let \(x_1(t_1)\leq c\leq x_2(t_1)\), and assume that there is a solution \(y\) defined on \([t_0,t_1]\) with \(y(t_1)=c\). Show that there exists a solution \(x\) on \([t_0,t_1]\) such that \(x(t_0)=x_0\) and \(x(t_1)=c\). Conclude that the set of values at \(t_1\) of all the solutions through \((t_0,x_0)\) defined on \([t_0,t_1]\) is an interval. (Hint: Let \(\tau\) be the smallest number such that \(x_1\leq y\leq x_2\) on \([\tau,t_1]\). If \(\tau=t_0\), then \(y(t_0)=x_0\); otherwise glue \(y\) on \([\tau,t_1]\) with \(x_1\) or \(x_2\) on \([t_0,\tau]\), and use Lemma 2.7.)

An additional argument shows that the condition \(\delta<1/L_P\) in the proof of the main theorem is superfluous. In fact, one can always take \(\delta=\min\{a,b/M\}\).

Exercise 2.16. Let \(Y\) be the set of continuous functions from \([t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta]\) to \(\R^k\) with the norm \[ {\lVert \bs g\rVert }_w=\max_{{\lvert t-t_0\rvert }\leq \delta}\left\{e^{-2L_P{\lvert t-t_0\rvert }}{\lvert \bs g(t)\rvert }\right\}. \]

Show that this norm is equivalent to \({\lVert \cdot\rVert }_\infty\), and hence \(Y\) is a Banach space.

Let \(\mathcal B\subseteq Y\) be the set of all functions \(\bs x\in Y\) such that \({\lvert \bs x(t)-\bs x_0\rvert }\leq b\) for all \(t\in[t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta]\). Use this norm to establish that \(T\colon \mathcal B\longrightarrow \mathcal B\) is a contraction on \(\mathcal B\) if \(\delta=\min\{a,b/M\}\). (All the constants here are as in the proof of the main theorem.)

In the following I will be using the main theorem in this specific form, assuming that \(\delta\) can always be chosen as \(\min\{a,b/M\}\). Note, however, that this estimate for the interval on which the solution exists and is unique is rather conservative.

Consider, e.g., \(\dot x=x^2,\,x(0)=1\). If I put an upper bound \(b\) on \({\lvert x-1\rvert }\), then \(M=\max_{x:{\lvert x-1\rvert }\leq b}x^2=(b+1)^2\), therefore, the solution is defined on \({\lvert t\rvert }\leq \delta\), where \[ \delta=\min\left\{\infty,\frac{b}{(b+1)^2}\right\}=\frac{b}{(b+1)^2}\,, \] which is equal, e.g., to \(2/9\) for \(b=2\), i.e., for the solution with the upper bound 3. The exact solution is \[ x(t)=\frac{1}{1-t}\,, \] and reaches the same upper bound at \(t=2/3\). The largest half-length of the interval that the theorem can provide is \(1/4\), attained at \(b=1\). Compare it with the exact solution, which is defined on \((-\infty,1)\).

The main theorem can be reformulated (rather straightforwardly) for equations and systems of arbitrary order.

Exercise 2.17. Formulate carefully the existence and uniqueness theorems for a \(k\)-th order ODE and for a system of \(m\) ODE of \(k\)-th order.

Comparing the statements of the main theorem and Theorem 2.44 immediately raises the question of whether it is possible to find weaker conditions on \(\bs f\) to guarantee uniqueness. The answer is positive; here is one known result along these lines. Let \(u\mapsto\phi(u)\) be continuous for \(u\geq 0\), \(\phi(0)=0\), \(\phi(u)>0\) for \(0<u<u_0\), and \[ \int_0^a\frac{\D u}{\phi(u)}=\infty \] for any \(0<a<u_0\). Assume that the continuous function \(\bs f\) is such that \[ {\lvert \bs f(t,\bs x_1)-\bs f(t,\bs x_2)\rvert }\leq \phi({\lvert \bs x_1-\bs x_2\rvert }) \] for \((t,\bs x_1),\,(t,\bs x_2)\) from \(U\). Then the solution to problem (2.7), which exists by Theorem 2.44, is unique for any \((t_0,\bs x_0)\in U\). This is Osgood’s theorem4. There are infinitely many such functions, e.g., \[ Lu,\quad Lu{\lvert \ln u\rvert },\quad Lu{\lvert \ln u\rvert }{\bigl\lvert \ln{\lvert \ln u\rvert }\bigr\rvert },\quad\ldots. \] (Note that the first choice here corresponds to the main case, when \(\bs f\) is assumed to be Lipschitz.)

From the point of view of applications, the right-hand side \(\bs f\) is often not continuous. For example, in control theory it is natural to consider an equation of the form \[ \dot x=f(t,x)+u(t)\eqqcolon g(t,x), \] where \(u\) is a control function, which can take only two values, say, \(+1\) and \(-1\). In this case the right-hand side of the differential equation is not even continuous, and it is not immediately clear what to call a solution. A usual approach is to replace the ODE with the corresponding integral equation \[ x(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^tg(\tau,x(\tau))\D \tau, \] and call a continuous solution to the integral equation a “solution” to the original ODE (it does not have to be differentiable at each point anymore). It can be proved that if \(g\) is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the second argument and, for each fixed \(x\), \(g\) is piecewise continuous as a function of \(t\), with discontinuities in \(t\) at points independent of \(x\), then the solution to the IVP (understood as a continuous solution to the integral equation) exists and is unique. It is possible to make further generalizations, but this would require the notion of Lebesgue’s integral (solutions in this sense are called Carathéodory solutions).

The following exercises check the basic understanding of the main theorem; do not skip them even if they look simple.

Can the graphs of two solutions to \(\ddot x=t+x^2\) cross at some point \((t_0,x_0)\) in the \((t,x)\)-plane?   1. This central theorem is often called the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, after Émile Picard, who developed the method of successive approximations around 1890, and Ernst Lindelöf, who in 1894 gave a concise exposition of Picard’s method for ordinary differential equations. Cauchy was also the first to publish a proof of existence and uniqueness, in the 1830s, albeit for analytic right-hand sides.↩︎

  2. After Giuseppe Peano, who proved it for a single equation in 1886 and for systems in 1890.↩︎

  3. After Hellmuth Kneser, a student of David Hilbert, who proved it in 1923 in a paper with the telling title “Über die Lösungen eines Systems gewöhnlicher Differentialgleichungen, das der Lipschitzschen Bedingung nicht genügt” (On the solutions of a system of ordinary differential equations that does not satisfy the Lipschitz condition).↩︎
