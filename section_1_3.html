1.3.1Separable equations

Definition 1.21. A first-order ordinary differential equation of the form \[ \dot x=f_1(t)f_2(x),\quad t\in J\subseteq \R,\quad x(t)\in X\subseteq \R \] is called separable.

From the previous section, the solution to a separable equation is given by \[ \int\frac{\D x}{f_2(x)}=\int f_1(t)\D t, \] if \(f_2(x)\neq 0\). If \(\hat{x}\) is such that \(f_2(\hat{x})=0\) then there is also the solution \(t\mapsto \hat{x}\). The existence and uniqueness of solutions are determined by the properties of \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) (Lemma 1.19).

1.3.2Linear equations

Definition 1.22. A first-order ordinary differential equation of the form \[ \dot x+p(t)x=q(t),\quad p,q\in\mathcal C(J) \] is called linear. If \(q\equiv 0\) then it is called linear homogeneous, whereas in the opposite case it is called nonhomogeneous.

Lemma 1.23. Assume that \(p,q\in\mathcal C(J)\) in the linear ODE. Then there exists a unique solution to the IVP with \(x(t_0)=x_0,\,t_0\in J\) defined on the whole interval \(J\).

I will give a constructive proof of this lemma by actually presenting the solution by the method called the variation of the parameter or variation of the constant.

Proof. First, consider the homogeneous linear equation \[ \dot x=-p(t)x, \] which is separable and can be integrated to get \[ x(t)=x_0e^{-\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}. \]

Now let us look for the solution to the full nonhomogeneous linear ODE in the form \begin{equation} x(t)=C(t)e^{-\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}, \tag{1.8}\end{equation} where \(t\mapsto C(t)\) is some unknown function of \(t\) (compare with the above solution to the homogeneous equation). Note that there is no restriction on the form of \(x\) in (1.8) since \(C\) is arbitrary and exponential never vanishes, so I just picked a form convenient for the computations without losing any generality. Next I show that it will be a solution if and only if \[ C'(t)=q(t)e^{\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}. \]

Indeed, assume (1.8) is a solution. After plugging this expression into the equation I get \[ C'(t)e^{-\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}-p(t)C(t)e^{-\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}+p(t)C(t)e^{-\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}=q(t)\Longleftrightarrow C'(t)=q(t)e^{\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}. \] Conversely, assume that \(C'(t)=q(t)e^{\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}\), then (1.8) is a solution by direct substitution.

Clearly the condition \(x(t_0)=x_0\) and assumption (1.8) are equivalent to \(C(t_0)=x_0\). Therefore \[ C(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^t q(\tau)e^{\int_{t_0}^\tau p(\xi)\D \xi}\D\tau. \]

Putting everything together I conclude that \[ x(t)=x_0e^{-\int_{t_0}^{t} p(\tau)\D \tau}+e^{-\int_{t_0}^{t} p(\tau)\D \tau}\int_{t_0}^t q(\tau)e^{\int_{t_0}^{\tau} p(\xi)\D \xi}\D \tau, \] is the unique solution to my IVP defined on the whole interval \(J\). ■

Remark 1.24. Another way to approach first-order linear equations is to use the so-called integrating factor \(\mu\). Specifically, note that if I define \[ \mu(t)\coloneqq\exp\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau, \] then my equation can be written as \[ (\mu(t)x(t))'=q(t)\mu(t), \] from which the same general solution immediately follows by integrating.

Remark 1.25. The solution to the linear ODE that I found above is often written with indefinite integrals as \[ x(t)=Ce^{-\int p(t)\D t}+e^{-\int p(t)\D t}\int q(t)e^{\int p(t)\D t}\D t. \] None of these formulas for the solution should be memorized, of course. It is much more efficient just to remember the steps: solve the homogeneous part, assume that the constant is a function, and determine this function.

Example 1.26. Solve \[ (t+x^2)\dot x=x. \] At first glance this problem is not linear. However, I can again play with the derivative as a fraction: \[ (t+x^2)\frac{\D x}{\D t}=x\Longleftrightarrow \frac{\D t}{\D x}-\frac{t}{x}=x, \] i.e., this equation is of the form \[ t'+p(x)t=q(x). \] (A rigorous justification of this manipulation can be made with the help of the inverse function theorem, see also Remark 1.11.) Now \[ \frac{\D t}{t}=\frac{\D x}{x}\Longleftrightarrow \log {\lvert t\rvert }=\log {\lvert Cx\rvert }\Longleftrightarrow t=Cx, \] and I point out that the arbitrary constant \(C\) can be different in different parts of the equality (as it happened in the previous line). Now I look for the solution in the form \(t=C(x)x\) and get \[ C'(x)=1\Longleftrightarrow C(x)=x+A, \] which implies that \(t=x^2+Ax\) is the general solution to the original equation, where \(A\) is an arbitrary constant.

1.3.3Exact equations

Going back to the geometric interpretation of the first-order ODE (and especially Remark 1.11), I remark that if I consider \[ \frac{\D x}{\D t}=f(t,x), \] then I actually ignore the directions parallel to the \(x\)-axis (the slope is infinite). If, as in Example 1.26, I take the point of view that \[ \frac{\D t}{\D x}=h(t,x), \] then I ignore the directions parallel to the \(t\)-axis.

There is a general way to take care of both of these directions by writing the first-order ODE in the symmetric form \[ M(t,x)\D t+N(t,x)\D x=0, \] where \(M\) and \(N\) are some given functions. Now the direction is not defined only at those points \((t,x)\) at which both \(M\) and \(N\) vanish.

Definition 1.27. A first-order ODE of the form \[ M(t,x)\D t+N(t,x)\D x=0 \] is called exact if there is a function \(F\colon U\subseteq \R^2\longrightarrow \R\), \(F\in \mathcal C^{(1)}(U)\) such that \[ \D F=M(t,x)\D t+N(t,x)\D x. \]

Here \(\D F\) is the total differential of the function of two variables, which is defined (let us take this definition at a formal level and not worry too much about what \(\D t\) and \(\D x\) actually mean) to be \[ \D F=\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}\D t+\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\D x. \] From the definition I immediately have that the general solution to the exact equation is given by \[ F(t,x)=\text{const}. \] From analysis I know that if \(U\) is “nice” (an open simply connected subset of the plane, i.e., without “holes”) then a necessary and sufficient condition for the equation to be exact is \[ \frac{\partial M}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial t} \] for all points \((t,x)\in U\). In this case the function \(F\) can be found by integration.

Exercise 1.31. Check that the following differential equation is exact and find its solution: \[ 3x^2(1+\log y)\D x=\left(2y-\frac{x^3}{y}\right)\D y. \]

Remark 1.28. In some sense exactness is what allows one to integrate an ODE in a closed form. Clearly, a separable equation \(\dot x=f_1(t)f_2(x)\) written as \[ f_1(t)\D t-\frac{1}{f_2(x)}\D x=0 \] is exact (I of course assume that \(f_2\neq 0\)).

The linear equation \(\dot x+p(t)x=q(t)\) is not exact, but it becomes exact after multiplication by an integrating factor \(\mu(t)=e^{\int_{t_0}^t p(\tau)\D \tau}\). Indeed, the ODE \[ \mu(t)\D x+(\mu(t)p(t)x-q(t)\mu(t))\D t=0 \] is exact (check it).

1.3.4Substitutions

Most ODE are neither separable, nor linear, nor exact. However, it is often possible to come up with a substitution that turns a given first-order ODE into one of these three types. Here are just two examples.

Definition 1.29. A first-order ODE of the form \[ \dot x=f\left(\frac{x}{t}\right) \] is called homogeneous.

The substitution \(x(t)=u(t)t\) turns it into a separable equation for the new unknown function \(u\).

Remark 1.30. I already used the term “homogeneous” for the linear homogeneous ODE \(\dot x+p(t)x=0\). Here the same word is used to refer to a different object, so be careful. The two are loosely related through the fact that the equations do not change after certain scalings of the variables, but these scalings are different (try to figure them out).

Definition 1.31. A first-order ODE of the form \[ \dot x+p(t)x=q(t)x^n,\quad n\in\R\setminus\{0,1\} \] is called Bernoulli’s equation.

The substitution \(u(t)=x^{1-n}(t)\) turns it into a linear equation for the new unknown function \(u\). I also note that for \(n\) non-integer I must require \(x>0\) and that for \(n>0\) after the substitution the solution \(x(t)=0\) is lost.

Modern computer algebra systems, like Maple or Mathematica, can solve a great many ODE in explicit form.

1.3.5Liouville’s theorem

Can I always find an analytical solution (a formula) to a first-order ODE? The answer is negative, as it was proved by Joseph Liouville in 18391. To be precise, he showed that there are some specific second-order linear differential equations with non-constant coefficients that cannot be solved in elementary functions. To wit, the following theorem2 holds.

Theorem 1.32 (Liouville). The solutions of the equation \(y''+xy=0\) cannot be obtained from the field of rational functions of \(x\) by any sequence of finite algebraic extensions, adjunctions of integrals, and adjunctions of exponentials of integrals.

It turns out that, after a specific substitution, Riccati’s equation \[ \dot x=q_0(t)+q_1(t)x+q_2(t)x^2 \] can be rewritten as a second-order linear ODE (I assume that \(q_2\in \mathcal C^{(1)},\,q_2\neq 0\)). First it can be rewritten, using the new variable \(v=q_2(t)x\), as \[ \dot v=v^2+p_1(t)v+p_2(t), \] and after this, using the substitution \(v=-{\dot u}/{u}\), as \[ \ddot u-p_1(t)\dot u+p_2(t)u=0, \] which is a second-order linear ODE. If I take \(\dot x=x^2+t\) then the corresponding second-order linear equation is \[ \ddot u+tu=0, \] for which Liouville’s theorem holds. If I was able to find an analytical solution for \(x\), it would mean that \(u\) could also be written as a formula, which would contradict Liouville’s theorem.
