Returning to the interpretation of the system of ordinary differential equations as a mathematical formalization of a finite-dimensional deterministic evolutionary process, it should be clear that existence and uniqueness of solutions are not the only desirable properties of solutions. It is also reasonable to expect that solutions will depend continuously on the initial conditions and parameters of the system. Taken together, these properties define what is called a well-posed problem1.

Definition 2.52. A problem is called well-posed if it has a solution, the solution is unique, and the solution depends continuously on the initial data and parameters of the problem.

2.6.1Continuous dependence on the initial conditions and parameters

The goal of this subsection is to prove that problem (2.7) is well-posed under the assumptions of Theorem 2.42.

The problem (2.7) does not include any parameters. This is not a restriction, because any system that depends explicitly on parameters, such as \[ \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x,\bs \mu),\quad \bs \mu\in \R^m,\quad \bs x(t_0)=\bs x_0, \] can be reformulated as \[ \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x,\bs y),\quad \dot{\bs y}=0,\quad \bs x(t_0)=\bs x_0,\quad \bs y(t_0)=\bs \mu, \] i.e., in the form (2.7). Conversely, if one is given a system in the form (2.7), then by introducing \(\bs y=\bs x-\bs x_0\) I find a new system \[ \dot{\bs y}=\bs f(t,\bs y+\bs x_0)=\bs g(t,\bs y,\bs x_0),\quad \bs y(t_0)=0, \] i.e., I can talk about the initial data and parameters interchangeably. Note, however, that to apply the theory to the extended system, the function \(\bs f\) must be (locally) Lipschitz with respect to \((\bs x,\bs \mu)\), and not only with respect to \(\bs x\).

First I will need Grönwall’s inequality2.

Lemma 2.53 (Grönwall’s inequality). Let \(u,a,b\) be continuous nonnegative functions on \([t_0,t_1]\), \(C\geq 0\), and \[ u(t)\leq C+\int_{t_0}^t\bigl(a(\tau)u(\tau)+b(\tau)\bigr)\D\tau,\quad t\in[t_0,t_1]. \] Then \[ u(t)\leq \left(C+\int_{t_0}^tb(\tau)\D\tau\right)\exp\left(\int_{t_0}^ta(\tau)\D\tau\right),\quad t\in[t_0,t_1]. \]

Proof. Denote by \(R(t)\) the right-hand side of the assumed inequality. Then \(R\) is continuously differentiable, \(u\leq R\), and \(R'=au+b\leq aR+b\). Hence, with \(E(t)=\exp\left(-\int_{t_0}^ta(\tau)\D\tau\right)\), \[ (RE)'=(R'-aR)E\leq bE\leq b. \] Integrating from \(t_0\) to \(t\) and using \(R(t_0)=C\), I get \(R(t)E(t)\leq C+\int_{t_0}^tb(\tau)\D\tau\), which together with \(u\leq R\) is the required inequality. ■

An analogous statement holds on an interval \([t_1,t_0]\) to the left of \(t_0\), with all the integrals taken from \(t\) to \(t_0\).

Now I am prepared to prove one more corollary to Theorem 2.48 that is often useful in estimating the interval of existence.

Corollary 2.54. Assume that \(U=J\times\R^k\), \(J=(c,d)\), and that there are continuous nonnegative functions \(A\) and \(B\) on \(J\) such that \[ {\lvert \bs f(t,\bs x)\rvert }\leq A(t){\lvert \bs x\rvert }+B(t),\quad (t,\bs x)\in U. \] Then every solution to (2.7) is global.

Proof. Let \(\bs\phi\) be the maximal solution, defined on \((\alpha,\beta)\), and assume that \(\beta<d\). By Lemmas 2.7 and 2.36, \[ {\lvert \bs \phi(t)\rvert }\leq {\lvert \bs x_0\rvert }+\int_{t_0}^t\bigl(A(\tau){\lvert \bs \phi(\tau)\rvert }+B(\tau)\bigr)\D \tau,\quad t\in[t_0,\beta), \] and Lemma 2.53 yields \[ {\lvert \bs \phi(t)\rvert }\leq R\coloneqq\left({\lvert \bs x_0\rvert }+\int_{t_0}^\beta B(\tau)\D\tau\right)\exp\left(\int_{t_0}^\beta A(\tau)\D\tau\right),\quad t\in[t_0,\beta). \] Hence the graph of \(\bs\phi\) over \([t_0,\beta)\) stays in the compact set \([t_0,\beta]\times\{{\lvert \bs x\rvert }\leq R\}\subseteq U\), which contradicts Theorem 2.48. Therefore \(\beta=d\), and similarly \(\alpha=c\), i.e., \(\bs\phi\) is global. ■

Finally, consider again the autonomous system \begin{equation} \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(\bs x),\quad\bs x(t)\in \R^k, \tag{2.8}\end{equation} defined on all \(\R^k\). I know that it is possible to have solutions not defined globally. Together with (2.8) consider \begin{equation} \dot{\bs x}=\frac{\bs f(\bs x)}{1+{\lvert \bs f(\bs x)\rvert }}\,. \tag{2.9}\end{equation} For the last system I have that \[ {\lvert \dot{\bs x}\rvert }\leq 1, \] and therefore the conditions of the last corollary are satisfied (note that \(\bs f/(1+{\lvert \bs f\rvert })\) is locally Lipschitz together with \(\bs f\)), and the solutions to this system are defined globally. It is possible to show that if I have two vector fields \(\bs x\mapsto \bs f(\bs x)\) and \(\bs x\mapsto h(\bs x)\bs f(\bs x)\), where \(h(\bs x)>0\), then the orbits (the images of the corresponding solutions in the phase space) coincide, together with their orientation, which in a sense shows that I can always reparametrize the time in an autonomous system to guarantee that all the solutions are global.

Now I will finalize the proof that problem (2.7) is well-posed.

The first step is a simple quantitative estimate: as long as two solutions stay in a region where \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz, the distance between them can grow at most exponentially.

Lemma 2.55. Let \(\bs x_1\) and \(\bs x_2\) be two solutions to \(\dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x)\) defined on an interval \(I\ni t_0\), and assume that their graphs lie in a set where \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz in \(\bs x\) with constant \(L\). Then \[ {\lvert \bs x_1(t)-\bs x_2(t)\rvert }\leq{\lvert \bs x_1(t_0)-\bs x_2(t_0)\rvert }\,e^{L{\lvert t-t_0\rvert }},\quad t\in I. \]

Proof. Let \(t\geq t_0\) (the case \(t<t_0\) is similar) and \(v(t)={\lvert \bs x_1(t)-\bs x_2(t)\rvert }\). By Lemmas 2.7 and 2.36, \[ v(t)\leq v(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^t{\lvert \bs f(\tau,\bs x_1(\tau))-\bs f(\tau,\bs x_2(\tau))\rvert }\D\tau\leq v(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^tLv(\tau)\D\tau, \] and Lemma 2.53 with \(C=v(t_0)\), \(a\equiv L\), and \(b\equiv 0\) yields the required estimate. ■

Lemma 2.55 alone does not yet give continuous dependence: it assumes that the nearby solution exists on the whole interval and stays in the region where \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz. The next theorem shows that, under the standing assumptions, both conditions hold automatically, and hence the flow of an autonomous system is continuous.

Theorem 2.56. Let \(X\subseteq\R^k\) be open, and let \(\bs f\colon X\longrightarrow\R^k\) be continuous and locally Lipschitz in \(X\). For \(\bs x_0\in X\) denote by \(t\mapsto\bs\phi(t,\bs x_0)\) the maximal solution to \[ \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(\bs x),\quad \bs x(0)=\bs x_0, \] defined on the maximal interval \(I(\bs x_0)=(\alpha(\bs x_0),\beta(\bs x_0))\). Then the set \[ D=\{(t,\bs x_0)\colon \bs x_0\in X,\ t\in I(\bs x_0)\} \] is open in \(\R\times\R^k\), and \(\bs\phi\colon D\longrightarrow X\) is continuous.

Proof. Fix \((t,\bs x_0)\in D\), and let \(t\geq 0\) (the case \(t<0\) is similar). Choose \(T\) such that \(t<T<\beta(\bs x_0)\).

The set \(\Gamma=\{\bs\phi(\tau,\bs x_0)\colon \tau\in[0,T]\}\) is compact, and since \(X\) is open, there is \(r>0\) such that the compact set \(K=\{\bs x\colon {\lvert \bs x-\bs z\rvert }\leq r\text{ for some }\bs z\in\Gamma\}\) lies in \(X\). By Lemma 2.40, \(\bs f\) is Lipschitz in \(K\) with some constant \(L\).

Let \(\bs y\in X\) satisfy \({\lvert \bs y-\bs x_0\rvert }<\rho\coloneqq re^{-LT}\). As long as \(\bs\phi(\tau,\bs y)\in K\), Lemma 2.55 gives \[ {\lvert \bs\phi(\tau,\bs y)-\bs\phi(\tau,\bs x_0)\rvert }\leq{\lvert \bs y-\bs x_0\rvert }e^{L\tau}<r,\quad \tau\in[0,T], \] so \(\bs\phi(\tau,\bs y)\) cannot reach the boundary of \(K\) and stays in \(K\) as long as it is defined on \([0,T]\). If \(\beta(\bs y)\leq T\), the graph of \(\bs\phi(\cdot,\bs y)\) over \([0,\beta(\bs y))\) would stay in the compact set \([0,T]\times K\), which contradicts Theorem 2.48. Hence \(\bs\phi(\tau,\bs y)\) is defined on \([0,T]\) and \[ {\lvert \bs\phi(\tau,\bs y)-\bs\phi(\tau,\bs x_0)\rvert }\leq{\lvert \bs y-\bs x_0\rvert }e^{LT},\quad \tau\in[0,T]. \]

Applying the same argument to the left of \(0\), I find \(\epsilon>0\) such that \((-\epsilon,T)\times B_\rho(\bs x_0)\subseteq D\), so \(D\) is open. Finally, for \((s,\bs y)\) in this neighborhood, \[ {\lvert \bs\phi(s,\bs y)-\bs\phi(t,\bs x_0)\rvert }\leq{\lvert \bs\phi(s,\bs y)-\bs\phi(s,\bs x_0)\rvert }+{\lvert \bs\phi(s,\bs x_0)-\bs\phi(t,\bs x_0)\rvert }\leq {\lvert \bs y-\bs x_0\rvert }e^{LT}+{\lvert \bs\phi(s,\bs x_0)-\bs\phi(t,\bs x_0)\rvert }, \] and both terms tend to zero as \((s,\bs y)\to(t,\bs x_0)\). ■

Remark 2.57. If \(\bs f\) is continuous and locally Lipschitz with respect to \((\bs x,\bs\mu)\), then applying Theorem 2.56 to the system \(\dot{\bs x}=\bs f(\bs x,\bs y)\), \(\dot{\bs y}=0\), I get that the solution \(\bs\phi(t,\bs x_0,\bs\mu)\) is continuous in \((t,\bs x_0,\bs\mu)\) on its (open) domain of definition. For a non-autonomous system \(\dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x)\) the proof above can be repeated verbatim.

Remark 2.58. In fact, for a continuous right-hand side the Lipschitz condition is not needed for continuous dependence: if \(\bs f\) is continuous and the solution to every initial value problem for \(\dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x)\) is unique, then the solutions depend continuously on the initial data. The proof uses the same compactness arguments as the proof of Peano’s theorem (see Hartman’s book). Thus, for ordinary differential equations with continuous right-hand side, uniqueness of solutions already implies well-posedness.

2.6.2Differentiable dependence on the initial conditions and parameters

It is also useful to know that if \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(p)}\), then the solution to the problem (2.7) depends differentiably on the initial conditions. Since the initial conditions and parameters of the problem can be interchanged, a similar statement applies to a parameter dependent problem. In particular, it is true that3

Theorem 2.59. Let \(p\geq 1\) and \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(p)}(U;\R^k)\), \(U=J\times X\times \Omega\). Then the problem \(\dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x,\bs \mu),\,\bs x(t_0)=\bs x_0\) has a solution \((t,t_0,\bs x_0,\bs \mu)\mapsto\bs \phi(t,t_0,\bs x_0,\bs \mu)\) that is \(p\) times continuously differentiable with respect to the variables \((t,t_0,\bs x_0,\bs \mu)\) on its (open) domain of definition.

In particular, if \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(1)}\), then differentiating the corresponding integral equation (2.6) with respect to the \(j\)-th coordinate \(x_{0j}\) of \(\bs x_0\) shows that \(\bs y=\partial\bs\phi/\partial x_{0j}\) solves the variational equation \begin{equation} \dot{\bs y}=\bs f_{\bs x}(t,\bs\phi(t))\,\bs y,\quad \bs y(t_0)=\bs e_j, \tag{2.10}\end{equation} where \(\bs f_{\bs x}\) is the Jacobi matrix of \(\bs f\) with respect to \(\bs x\), and \(\bs e_j\) is the \(j\)-th standard basis vector. Similarly, \(\bs z=\partial\bs\phi/\partial\mu_j\) solves \(\dot{\bs z}=\bs f_{\bs x}(t,\bs\phi(t),\bs\mu)\,\bs z+\partial\bs f/\partial\mu_j(t,\bs\phi(t),\bs\mu)\), \(\bs z(t_0)=0\). Note that the variational equation is linear.

This theorem is important because it justifies some of the basic perturbation methods. Assume that a problem of the form \[ \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x,\epsilon),\quad \bs x(0)=\bs \eta \] is given, depending on the scalar (for simplicity) parameter \(\epsilon\). Assume that \(\bs f\in\mathcal C^{(p+1)}\). Then the solution has \(p+1\) continuous derivatives with respect to this parameter; in particular, for \(t\) in any closed bounded interval on which the solution with \(\epsilon=0\) is defined, the solution can be represented by the Taylor formula \[ \bs x(t)=\bs x_0(t)+\bs x_1(t)\epsilon+\bs x_2(t)\epsilon^2+\ldots+\bs x_p(t)\epsilon^p+\mathcal O({\lvert \epsilon\rvert }^{p+1}), \] where \(\bs x_i(t)=\frac{1}{i!}\frac{\partial^i\bs x}{\partial \epsilon^i}(t)\big|_{\epsilon=0}\), and the constant in \(\mathcal O({\lvert \epsilon\rvert }^{p+1})\) does not depend on \(t\) in this interval. Here \(g=\mathcal O(f)\) as \(u\to a\) means that \({\lvert g(u)\rvert }\leq C{\lvert f(u)\rvert }\) for some constant \(C\) and \({\lvert u-a\rvert }< \delta\). Hence \(\bs x_0\) is the solution of the unperturbed problem \[ \dot{\bs x}_0=\bs f(t,\bs x_0,0)=\bs f_0(t,\bs x_0),\quad \bs x_0(0)=\bs \eta. \] To find the equations to determine other \(\bs x_i\) I can either formally differentiate the original equation with respect to \(\epsilon\), or, simpler, plug \(\bs x\) into the original equation, expand the corresponding series, and equate the coefficients of the same powers of \(\epsilon\).

Example 2.60. Consider the IVP \[ \ddot x+\omega^2x=\epsilon x^3,\quad x(0)=1,\quad \dot x(0)=0, \] where \(\omega>0\) is a constant. Let me find \(\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial \epsilon}(t,\epsilon)\right|_{\epsilon=0}=x_1(t)\). First, I note that if \(\epsilon=0\) then I have \[ \ddot x_0+\omega^2 x_0=0,\quad x_0(0)=1,\quad \dot x_0(0)=0, \] with the solution \[ x_0(t)=\cos \omega t. \] Differentiating the original equation with respect to \(\epsilon\) I find \[ \ddot x_1+\omega^2x_1=\cos^3\omega t=\frac 14(\cos 3\omega t+3\cos \omega t), \] with the initial conditions \[ x_1(0)=0,\quad \dot x_1(0)=0. \] The last problem can be solved to find that \[ x_1(t)=\left.\frac{\partial x}{\partial \epsilon}(t)\right|_{\epsilon=0}=\frac{3}{8\omega}t\sin \omega t+\frac{1}{32\omega^2}(\cos\omega t-\cos 3\omega t). \] Finally, I can use the found expression to approximate the unknown solution to the original problem as \[ x(t)=x_0(t)+x_1(t)\epsilon+\mathcal O({\lvert \epsilon\rvert }^2). \] Note that \(x_1\) contains the secular term \(\frac{3}{8\omega}t\sin\omega t\), which grows with \(t\). Hence this approximation is good only on bounded time intervals: for \(t\) of order \(1/\epsilon\) the correction \(\epsilon x_1\) is of the same order as \(x_0\). Approximations valid on long time intervals require other methods (e.g., the Lindstedt–Poincaré method).

In the following exercises the variational equation (2.10) will be useful.

Exercise 2.30. Find the derivative of the solution to \(\dot x=x^2+x\sin t\) with respect to the initial condition \(x(0)=a\) at \(a=0\).

Answer: \(e^{1-\cos t}\).

Exercise 2.31. Find the derivative of the solution to the pendulum equation \(\ddot \theta+\sin \theta=0\) with the initial condition \(\theta(0)=a,\,\dot \theta(0)=0\) with respect to \(a\) at \(a=0\).

Answer: \(\cos t\).

Exercise 2.32. Let \[ \dot x=f(t,x),\quad x(\tau)=a, \] and assume that the solution \(x=x(t,\tau,a)=x(t,\tau)\). Assume also that the solution at a specific time moment \(\tau=t_0\) is known and given by \(t\mapsto x_0(t,t_0)\). Find the initial value problem for \[ x_1(t,t_0)=\frac{\partial x}{\partial \tau}(t,\tau)|_{\tau=t_0}. \]

Answer: \[ \dot x_1(t,t_0)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,x_0(t,t_0))x_1(t,t_0),\quad x_1(t_0,t_0)=-f(t_0,a). \]
