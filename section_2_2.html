Motivation for the proof of the existence and uniqueness theorem

Chapter 2 · Fundamental theorems

My first goal is to establish the fundamental theorem that states that if the right-hand side of the system \begin{equation} \dot{\bs x}=\bs f(t,\bs x),\quad \bs x(t)\in X\subseteq \R^k,\quad \bs f\colon J\times X\longrightarrow \R^k, \tag{2.4}\end{equation} is “good enough” (here, \(J\subseteq \R\) is an interval), then the initial value problem (2.4) and (2.5): \begin{equation} \bs x(t_0)=\bs x_0\in X,\quad t_0\in J, \tag{2.5}\end{equation} has a unique solution.

To make the exposition self-contained, let me first define a solution to problem (2.4), (2.5).

Definition 2.6. A solution to (2.4) on an interval \(I\subseteq J\) is a function \(\bs\phi\in\mathcal C^{(1)}(I;\R^k)\) such that \(\bs\phi(t)\in X\) and \(\dot{\bs\phi}(t)=\bs f(t,\bs\phi(t))\) for all \(t\in I\) (with one-sided derivatives at the endpoints of \(I\), if they belong to \(I\)). A solution to the initial value problem (2.4), (2.5) is a solution with \(t_0\in I\) and \(\bs\phi(t_0)=\bs x_0\).

A crucial step in establishing the existence and uniqueness (both at the same time!) of a solution is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7. Let \(U\subseteq J\times X\), \(\bs f\in \mathcal C(U;\R^k)\), and \((t_0,\bs x_0)\in U\). Let \(I\subseteq J\) be an interval containing \(t_0\), and let \(\bs x\colon I\longrightarrow X\) be such that \((t,\bs x(t))\in U\) for all \(t\in I\). Then \(\bs x\) solves problem (2.4), (2.5) on \(I\) if and only if \(\bs x\) is continuous on \(I\) and solves the integral equation \begin{equation} \bs x(t)=\bs x_0+\int_{t_0}^t \bs f(\tau,\bs x(\tau))\D \tau,\quad t\in I. \tag{2.6}\end{equation}

Proof. Let \(\bs x\) solve (2.4), (2.5) on \(I\) and let \(t\in I\). Then \(\bs x\) is continuous, and, by integrating (2.4) from \(t_0\) to \(t\) and taking into account (2.5), I get (2.6).

Now, let \(\bs x\) be a continuous solution to (2.6) on \(I\). Then I have \(\bs x(t_0)=\bs x_0\) by direct substitution. Since the graph of \(\bs x\) lies in \(U\), the function \(\tau\mapsto \bs f(\tau,\bs x(\tau))\) is continuous on \(I\) as a composition of continuous functions. Therefore, the right-hand side of (2.6) is continuously differentiable as an integral of a continuous function, and so is the left-hand side \(\bs x\). By differentiating both sides of (2.6) I obtain (2.4). ■

Now I can guess that at least sufficiently close to \(t_0\) the constant \(\bs x_0\) approximates the unknown \(\bs x\); therefore, \[ \bs x_1(t)=\bs x_0+\int_{t_0}^t \bs f(\tau,\bs x_0)\D \tau. \] It is my hope that \(\bs x_1\) approximates \(\bs x\) “better” than \(\bs x_0\). Or, in general, that the \(n\)-th iteration \[ \bs x_n(t)=\bs x_0+\int_{t_0}^t\bs f(\tau,\bs x_{n-1}(\tau))\D \tau \] gives “almost” the solution to the original problem.

The sequence1 \((\bs x_n)_{n=0}^\infty\) is called the sequence of Picard iterates2 and there is hope that this sequence converges to the unknown solution \(\bs x\) as the following example shows.

Example 2.8. Consider the IVP \[ \dot x=x,\quad x(0)=x_0. \] The Picard iterates take the form \begin{align*} x_1(t)&=x_0+x_0t=(1+t)x_0,\\ x_2(t)&=\left(1+t+\frac{t^2}{2}\right)x_0,\\ x_3(t)&=\left(1+t+\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^3}{3!}\right)x_0,\\ &\ldots\\ x_n(t)&=\left(1+\frac{t}{1!}+\frac{t^2}{2!}+\ldots+\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\right)x_0. \end{align*} These are the partial sums of the Taylor series for the exponential function \[ e^{t}=\sum_{i=0}^\infty \frac{t^i}{i!}\,, \] which converges uniformly on any compact interval of \(\R\) (I will discuss the uniform convergence in the next section). And indeed, as I already know (Lemma 1.8), \(t\mapsto x_0e^t\) solves my initial value problem.

It is actually possible to proceed in a similar way with the IVP (2.4), (2.5) by building the Picard iterates and proving that they, under some additional assumptions on \(\bs f\), converge uniformly to some function \(\bs x\) that, therefore, solves the integral equation (2.6). Since each Picard iterate is a continuous function by construction, the uniform convergence implies that \(\bs x\) is continuous, and hence, according to Lemma 2.7 above, solves (2.4), (2.5). However, I will take a more abstract route and present a conceptual proof that, first, shows exactly what is at work here, and, second, is widely applicable outside the area of ODE. For this proof I will need some basic notions of analysis.

Exercise 2.1. Find the first three Picard iterates for

  1. \[ \dot x=t-x^2,\quad x(0)=0.\]
  2. \[ \dot x_1=2t+x_2,\quad \dot x_2=x_1,\quad x_1(1)=1,\quad x_2(1)=0. \]

Exercise 2.2. Derive the Taylor series for \(x(t)=\sin t\) by applying the Picard method to the first-order system corresponding to \[ \ddot x=-x,\quad x(0)=0,\quad \dot x(0)=1. \]

  1. The correct notation \((\bs x_n)_{n=0}^\infty\) for the sequence \(\bs x_0,\bs x_1,\ldots\) will be often abbreviated as \((\bs x_n)\) in the rest of these notes.↩︎

  2. After Émile Picard who used this method of successive approximations systematically around 1890, first for partial and then for ordinary differential equations.↩︎
